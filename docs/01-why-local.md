# Why Run AI on Your Own Computer

Before the how, spend ten minutes on the why, because the why decides the how. Three reasons come up over and over: privacy, offline use, and not paying rent for intelligence.

## Reason 1: privacy

When you type into a typical cloud assistant, your words travel to someone else's server. The company promises rules about what happens next, but the mechanics stay out of your hands. Your text — your questions, your drafts, your personal notes — sits on hardware you do not control.

Running a local model flips that. The model is a file on your disk. Your prompt goes to your processor and comes back to your screen. No server sees the conversation, because no server is involved.

> The one-sentence version: with local AI, the cloud vendor learns nothing, because there is no cloud vendor in the loop.

This matters most for text you would not paste into a public form: family matters, business drafts, journaling, rough thinking you would not want attached to your name forever. Chapter 7 covers the fine print — local is hugely better for privacy, but it is not magical.

## Reason 2: offline

Once the model is downloaded, it runs with the network cable pulled. Trains, planes, rural houses, hotel Wi-Fi that blocks everything, the neighbor's internet outage at 9 pm: the assistant keeps working.

This is also a nice honesty test. Put your machine in airplane mode and send a chat. If it answers, it was truly local.

## Reason 3: no subscription

Cloud assistants rent you intelligence by the month. Local AI produces intelligence from electricity. A small model answering questions on a laptop costs a few cents of power per evening of heavy use.

There is a second, quieter part of this: local AI does not change under you. Subscription services update their behavior without asking. Your local model keeps answering the same way on the day it was downloaded for as long as the file exists on your disk.

## What you give up

Being honest builds trust: local models are smaller, slower, and less knowledgeable than the big cloud models. This book leans into that constraint. We pick modest models that fit old hardware, and we lean on the local model for drafting, summarizing, explaining, and chatting — while the cloud keeps its crown for giant research jobs.

A table of exactly where each side wins is in chapter 8: [When Local Beats the Cloud](08-local-vs-cloud.md).

## One personal note from the author

I spent years as a nurse, and the privacy lesson stuck early: some things simply should not leave the room. (Chapter 7 makes the health-care version of this explicit: never paste real patient details into *any* AI, local or cloud — local tools are for learning and drafting, never for medical decisions.)

That sensibility runs through this whole manual. The tools should be small enough to understand and private enough to trust.
